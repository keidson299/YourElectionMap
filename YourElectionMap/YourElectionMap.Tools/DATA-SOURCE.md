# Where the district boundaries come from

Every boundary on the site comes from the Census Bureau's TIGERweb map services: the districts
from `TIGERweb/Legislative/MapServer`, and the counties, cities and school districts from
`TIGERweb/State_County`, `TIGERweb/Places_CouSub_ConCity_SubMCD` and `TIGERweb/School`. The
server reads one file per state from
`YourElectionMap.Server/Districts/Data/`, each generated by this tool and git-ignored. Every file
holds its rings in longitude and latitude, to six decimal places, thinned to 15 feet.

Every state's folder holds its five local layers too, downloaded on the same day, apart from
those of Arizona, Idaho, Montana, Utah and Wyoming, downloaded on 2026-10-12. The seventeen
states with towns or townships of their own (see below) hold a sixth, their county subdivisions,
downloaded on 2026-10-26. Every folder holds the previous vintage's legislative layers as well,
downloaded on 2026-10-25 to check the current ones against (see **Are the plans current?**), and
Missouri's file is built from one of them.

## The source

TIGERweb's current layers are the January 1, 2026 vintage. TIGER data is public domain.

| Level | Service, layer | Saved as |
| --- | --- | --- |
| US House | `Legislative`, 0, 120th Congressional Districts | `cd.geojson` |
| State Senate | `Legislative`, 1, 2026 State Legislative Districts – Upper | `sldu.geojson` |
| State House / Assembly | `Legislative`, 2, 2026 State Legislative Districts – Lower | `sldl.geojson` |
| County | `State_County`, 1, Counties | `county.geojson` |
| City or town | `Places_CouSub_ConCity_SubMCD`, 4, Incorporated Places | `place.geojson` |
| Town or township | `Places_CouSub_ConCity_SubMCD`, 1, County Subdivisions | `cousub.geojson` |
| School district | `School`, 0, Unified School Districts | `unsd.geojson` |
| High school district | `School`, 1, Secondary School Districts | `scsd.geojson` |
| Elementary school district | `School`, 2, Elementary School Districts | `elsd.geojson` |

The three legislative layers are required, but for Nebraska's lower house, which it does not
have (see below). The local ones are optional: the tool builds
a state without any it does not find, and says so. Each service lists the same layers again
for earlier vintages further down, and `State_County` lists more county layers, which are
coarser copies for zoomed-out maps; layer 1 is the full-detail one. Census designated places
(layer 5 of the places service) are left out: they are named communities with no government of
their own, and nobody is elected to them.

Each layer is fetched with one request per state, in longitude and latitude (`outSR=4326`), with
the state's FIPS code in `STATE`:

```
https://tigerweb.geo.census.gov/arcgis/rest/services/TIGERweb/<service>/MapServer/<layer>/query?where=STATE%3D%27<FIPS>%27&outFields=NAME,GEOID&returnGeometry=true&outSR=4326&f=geojson
```

A state with no high school or elementary districts gets a file with no features in it, which
is fine: the school districts of Alabama, Arkansas, Colorado, Delaware, Florida, Idaho, Indiana,
Iowa, Kansas, Louisiana, Maryland, Mississippi, Nebraska, Nevada, New Mexico, North Carolina,
Ohio, Pennsylvania, South Carolina, South Dakota, Utah, Washington and West Virginia are all
unified.

A layer too big for one answer comes back with `"exceededTransferLimit": true`. Fetch it in
pages with `resultOffset` and `resultRecordCount`, and join the features into one file. Texas's
unified school districts are too big even to say so: the one request comes back empty. Fetch
them in pages from the start, 100 at a time, ordered with `orderByFields=GEOID`, and check the
total against the same query with `returnCountOnly=true`, which counts 1,016.

The county subdivisions need two fields more, `outFields=NAME,GEOID,FUNCSTAT,LSADC`.

### North Carolina

The 120th Congress's districts are the General Assembly's 2025 plan (CCM-2, enacted 2025-10-21);
the legislative layers are the 2023 plans (Session Laws 2023-146 and 2023-149). Until slice 9 the
site was built from those enacted plans' own shapefiles. Before the switch, TIGER was checked
against them on a 0.02° grid over the state: the US House layer agreed on every point but one,
the State Senate on all but two and the State House on all but two, each of those on a district
line. The General Assembly's PDF maps of the plans remain the thing to check a result against.

### California

The 120th Congress's districts are the map California's voters approved in November 2025
(Proposition 50), used from the 2026 elections. Layer 4, the 119th Congress's districts, is the
map before it. Saved as `Tiger\ca\cd-119.geojson`, it places 34% of a 0.02° grid over the state
in different districts from layer 0, which is how it was confirmed that layer 0 holds the new
map. The tool does not read it.

California has all three kinds of school district: 345 unified, and in the places without one, a
high school district over one or more elementary districts. A few of the high school districts
are a unified district's high school grades serving its neighbours, named for it with "(9-12)"
on the end, "Bishop Unified School District (9-12)". One unified "district" is TIGER's
"School District Not Defined", the land no district covers; the tool leaves it out.

### Oregon

Oregon's school districts are unified but for one corner of the south-east, where Harney County
Union High School District 1J sits over nine elementary districts. Oregon's school layer has a "School
District Not Defined" too, which the tool leaves out as it does California's.

### Arizona and Montana

Both have all three kinds of school district, as California does. Arizona has 97 unified
districts and, elsewhere, 15 union high school districts over 102 elementary districts; its
"School District Not Defined" is large, and leaves about one point in twenty of the test grid with
no school row. Montana has 97 high school districts over 233 elementary districts, such as
"Helena High School District" over "Helena Elementary School District", and 65 K-12 districts
besides.

### Oklahoma

Oklahoma has 91 elementary districts besides its 415 unified ones, and no high school districts:
TIGER puts nothing over them for the upper grades, so an address in one has its elementary
district as its only school row, as in Wyoming's Fremont County School District 38.

### Texas

The 120th Congress's districts are the map the Legislature redrew in 2025. Layer 4, the 119th
Congress's districts, is the map before it. Saved as `Tiger\tx\cd-119.geojson`, it differs from
layer 0 in 37 of the 38 districts' outlines, which is how it was confirmed that layer 0 holds the
new map. The tool does not read it.

Texas's school districts are unified but for one high school district over one elementary
district, "Hallettsville Independent School District (9-12) in Vysehrad" over "Vysehrad
Independent School District", in Lavaca County.

### Nevada

Nevada's lower house is the Assembly, as California's is, so its file names it "State Assembly".
Carson City is a county and a city in one, and TIGER names both "Carson City", so the capitol's
county and city rows read the same.

### Nebraska

Nebraska's Legislature has one chamber. TIGER holds its 49 districts in the upper layer, named
"State Senate District 28", and has nothing for Nebraska in the lower one, so its folder has no
`sldl.geojson` and `Tiger.cs` lists two layers for it. The file names the districts'
office "Legislature", which is what the ballot says ("Member of the Legislature"), and has no
`state-lower` rows; the page selects the Legislature district first where it would otherwise
select the State House one.

### Minnesota, North Dakota and South Dakota

Each elects its House from halves of Senate districts, at least in places, and TIGER names a half
"State House District 65B". Minnesota halves all 67 of its Senate districts, so its 134 House
districts run 1A, 1B to 67B; North Dakota halves District 4 alone, into 4A and 4B, and South
Dakota Districts 26 and 28. Their other House districts elect two representatives each from the
Senate district's outline, as Washington's do. `Tiger.cs` lists which districts are halved, and
the tool keeps the letter in the number, so the page shows "State House · District 65B".

North Dakota has 20 elementary districts and Minnesota 7 with no high school district over them in
TIGER, as in Oklahoma. Minnesota also has one high school district over one elementary district,
"Park Rapids Public School District in Pine Point (9-12)" over "Pine Point Public School District",
and a "School District Not Defined", which the tool leaves out. Both Dakotas have one House seat,
at large, numbered 1 as Wyoming's is.

### Louisiana

Louisiana's counties are parishes, and TIGER names them so, "East Baton Rouge Parish". `Tiger.cs`
gives Louisiana's county rows the detail "Parish" in place of "County"; their level and ids are
the same as any county's, "county-22033".

Its Senate layer has one feature more than its 39 districts: "State Senate Districts not
defined", GEOID `22ZZZ`, which is Lake Pontchartrain. No Senate district takes the lake in,
though the House districts, the parishes and the school districts reach across it. The tool
leaves the feature out, as it leaves out a school layer's "not defined" area, so a point on the
lake has no State Senate row. The tests allow that one missing row inside a box around the
lake, and nowhere else.

### Missouri

Missouri has 71 elementary districts besides its 445 unified ones, and no high school districts:
TIGER puts nothing over them for the upper grades, as in Oklahoma. St. Louis is a city outside
any county, so TIGER has it in the county layer as "St. Louis city" as well as in the place
layer, and its county and city rows read the same, as Carson City's do.

Its US House districts are not TIGER's current ones. The 120th Congress layer holds the map the
Legislature drew in 2025 (HB 1), which the Missouri Supreme Court and then the U.S. Supreme Court
kept out of the 2026 elections, pending a referendum on it, Proposition A, on the November 2026
ballot. The 2022 map is used instead, and that is the 119th Congress layer, layer 4, saved as
`Tiger\mo\cd-119.geojson`, which `Tiger.cs` reads in place of `cd.geojson`; the Jefferson City
capitol is in District 3. If Proposition A passes, the 2025 map is used from 2028, and `Tiger.cs`
should read `cd.geojson` again.

### Tennessee, Kentucky and Georgia

Tennessee has 15 towns with an elementary district of their own, such as "Franklin Special
School District", inside a high school district TIGER names for the county and the town,
"Williamson County School District in Franklin"; its other 126 districts are unified. Kentucky
has 4 such pairs, "Anchorage Independent School District" inside "Jefferson County School
District in Anchorage ISD" among them, and 169 unified districts. Georgia has one place:
"Fort Benning Schools", the post's elementary district, across two high school districts,
"Chattahoochee County for Fort Benning" and "Muscogee County for Fort Benning". An address in
any of them has a high school and an elementary row, as in Arizona.

Tennessee and Kentucky each have a "School District Not Defined", which the tool leaves out:
Tennessee's along the Mississippi by Memphis, and Kentucky's the Kentucky Bend, the state's
corner cut off by a loop of the Mississippi.

### The Great Lakes

TIGER's counties reach out over the Great Lakes to the state line, and Michigan's, Indiana's and
Ohio's legislative districts go with them. Illinois's do not: its US House, Senate and House
layers each have a "not defined" area, GEOID `17ZZ` in the US House layer and `17ZZZ` in the
other two, which is its part of Lake Michigan. Wisconsin's US House districts reach over its
lakes, but its Senate and Assembly districts stop at the shore: both layers have a "not defined"
area, `55ZZZ`, in five pieces, Lake Michigan with Green Bay, Lake Winnebago, Lake Superior, and
two in the harbor at Superior. The tool leaves all of these out, so a point out on the water
has a county and, in Wisconsin, a US House district, but no legislators. The tests allow that
inside a box around each piece, and only for those levels all together, which one district
gone missing would not do. Each of the five states' school layers has a "School District Not
Defined" as well, mostly the same water, which the tool leaves out.

Illinois has 105 high school districts over 371 elementary districts besides its 386 unified
ones, as California does, and Wisconsin 10 union high school districts over 43 elementary
districts besides its 368 unified ones. Michigan has 27 elementary districts with no high
school district over them in TIGER, as in Oklahoma.

### Maryland, Virginia and West Virginia

All three call their lower house the House of Delegates, so their files name it that. Maryland
elects three delegates from each of its 47 districts: from the whole district in 29, and in the
rest from parts TIGER names "State Legislative Subdistrict 2A", halves in 12 districts and thirds,
1A, 1B and 1C, in 6. `Tiger.cs` lists which are halved and which are in three, and its file has
71 House of Delegates districts. West Virginia's 17 Senate districts each elect two senators,
and its 100 House of Delegates districts one delegate each.

Virginia's 38 independent cities are in no county, as St. Louis is, and so is Baltimore: TIGER
has each in the county layer, "Richmond city", GEOID `51760`, as well as in the place layer, and
their county and city rows read the same. Virginia's school districts are unified but for
"Lexington City Public Schools", an elementary district with no high school district over it in
TIGER, as in Oklahoma.

The districts and counties of Maryland and Virginia reach across the Chesapeake Bay, and
Pennsylvania's across its part of Lake Erie, which only its school layer leaves out, as a
"School District Not Defined".

### Towns and townships

In seventeen of the states a town or township can govern itself, as a city does, with officers
its voters elect: Connecticut, Illinois, Indiana, Kansas, Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota, Missouri, Nebraska, New Jersey, New York, North Dakota, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, South Dakota and Wisconsin. TIGER keeps them in its county subdivisions,
`cousub.geojson`, and most of them are not places. In the other twenty-eight states the county
subdivisions are statistical areas with nothing to keep, so their folders do without the layer.

The tool keeps a county subdivision whose functional status, `FUNCSTAT`, is A, an active
government, or B, one that shares some of its work with another, 15,243 in all:

| State | Kept | State | Kept | State | Kept |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Connecticut | 149 towns | Minnesota | 1,776 townships | North Dakota | 1,305 townships |
| Illinois | 1,425 townships | Missouri | 283 townships | Ohio | 1,308 townships |
| Indiana | 1,003 townships | Nebraska | 319 townships | Pennsylvania | 1,543 townships |
| Kansas | 1,258 townships | New Jersey | 240 townships | Rhode Island | 31 towns |
| Massachusetts | 293 towns | New York | 930 towns | South Dakota | 898 townships |
| Michigan | 1,240 townships | | | Wisconsin | 1,242 towns |

It leaves out the rest: F, a city, borough or village the layer has again, which the places
give; C, a town that is one with its city, as Hartford is, whose row is the city's; G, New York
City's five boroughs; I and N, inactive or not functioning, as 1,094 of Missouri's townships
and Illinois's precincts are; and S, unorganized territory, as in Minnesota and the Dakotas.
The legal type, `LSADC`, gives the row's detail: 43 is "Town", and 44, 45 (Kansas's and
Nebraska's numbered "Township 4") and 49 (Michigan's charter townships) are "Township". Any other
type among those kept stops the tool.

Where a city or village is inside a township, as Michigan's, Ohio's and New York's villages, a
Connecticut borough and every city in Indiana and most of Illinois are, an address has both
rows, and the server lists the town or township first: "Capital township", then "Springfield
city". Elsewhere, in Pennsylvania, Wisconsin, Minnesota and New Jersey and for any city that
stands apart from the townships, an address has one. On a 0.05° grid the points with no city
row fall from 4,902 to 90 over Pennsylvania, 11,150 to 4,200 over Michigan, 7,280 to 846 over
Wisconsin and 8,914 to 4,927 over South Dakota; what is left is water, unorganized land and the
parts of Missouri, Nebraska and the Dakotas whose townships do not govern. The files grow by up
to 5 MB each, Pennsylvania's from 9 to 14.

Each county subdivision layer has "County subdivisions not defined" areas, water, which are F
and left out. Wisconsin's towns reach over Lake Winnebago, so a point on it has a town though no
legislators.

### Connecticut, Massachusetts, New Jersey, New York and Rhode Island

Connecticut has no county governments, and since 2022 TIGER has its nine planning regions in the
county layer instead, "Capitol Planning Region", GEOID `09110`; `Tiger.cs` gives their rows the
detail "Planning region".

Massachusetts names its legislative districts and numbers them only in the `GEOID`: TIGER's
Senate districts are "Third Suffolk District", `25D25`, and its House districts "8th Suffolk
District", `25130`. `Tiger.cs` marks both layers as named: each row's office is its name, its
detail its chamber, "State Senate", it has no number, and its id is its level and `GEOID`,
"state-upper-25D25", as a county's is. The tool checks a named layer has its 40 or 160 districts,
each once. New Jersey's 40 districts each elect a senator and two members of the General
Assembly, and TIGER has them in both layers, as Washington's.

Connecticut's districts of all three levels stop at the shore of Long Island Sound, GEOID `09ZZ`
and `09ZZZ`, and Massachusetts's House districts at its coast, `25ZZZ` in five pieces: Cape Cod
Bay and Nantucket Sound, Massachusetts Bay off Hull and off the North Shore, Buzzards Bay, and
Rhode Island Sound off Westport. The counties go on over the water, so a point there has a
county and, in Massachusetts, a US House and State Senate district. The tests allow that as
they do for the Great Lakes.

Their school layers have high school districts over elementary ones besides their unified
districts: Connecticut 8 regional districts and 44 elementary ones besides 113 unified,
Massachusetts 30 and 64 besides 211, New Jersey 45 and 168 besides 341, New York three central
high school districts on Long Island and 14 elementary ones besides 662, and Rhode Island
Foster-Glocester and 5 elementary ones besides 30.

### Washington, Arizona and Idaho

Each Legislature has one set of districts, 49 in Washington, 30 in Arizona and 35 in Idaho, each
electing one senator and two representatives. TIGER holds them in both legislative layers
("Legislative (Senate) District 22" and "Legislative (House) District 22" in Washington), so each
file has its State House districts twice over, with the same outlines as the State Senate's.

### Utah

The 120th Congress's districts are the map the state court chose in November 2025, used from
the 2026 elections. Layer 0 holds it: its District 1 lies wholly inside Salt Lake County, which
no district of the 2021 map did.

### Wyoming

Wyoming has one seat in the House, which TIGER names "Congressional District (at Large)", with
no number. The tool numbers it 1, so the page shows "U.S. House · District 1".

Its school districts are unified apart from one elementary district, Fremont County School
District 38, with no high school district over it in TIGER. Its school layer has a "School
District Not Defined" too, which leaves 207 points of 11,200 on the test grid with no school row.

## What the files hold

- **One feature per district**, named "Congressional District 7", "State Senate District 8",
  "State House District 38" or "Assembly District 6". A few are MultiPolygons, for islands. The
  tool takes the number from the end of `NAME`, with the letter of a halved district's half or a
  third, "State House District 4A", "State Legislative Subdistrict 1C", apart from the at-large districts of Wyoming and the Dakotas
  and Massachusetts's named ones, which have none. A
  layer's "not defined" area, GEOID `<FIPS>ZZ` in a US House layer and `<FIPS>ZZZ` in a
  legislative one, is left out: Louisiana's Senate, Illinois's and Connecticut's three layers,
  Wisconsin's two chambers and Massachusetts's House have one, for water.
- **`GEOID`**, which starts with the state's FIPS code.
- **One feature per county, city or school district**, named in full by `NAME`, the way the
  Census writes it: "Wake County", "Raleigh city", "Wake County Schools", "Central Union High
  School District". These have no number. The tool stores each with its name as the office,
  no number, and an id made from its level and `GEOID`, "county-37183", which is stable and
  safe in the page's URL. A school layer's "School District Not Defined" is left out, so an
  address there has no school row. Cities are incorporated places, and towns and townships the
  county subdivisions that govern themselves, so an address outside both, in unincorporated
  county land, has no city row either.
- **No population.** TIGERweb's `POP100` field is empty for these layers, so the files have none
  and the map chip shows none.
- **Longitude and latitude, WGS84,** which is what `outSR=4326` asks for. The tool thins each ring to 15 feet, taken as degrees of
  latitude, which keeps slightly more than that east to west.

In every state the three layers cover the state exactly once each, but for the water TIGER
leaves out, Lake Pontchartrain in Louisiana, the lakes in Illinois and Wisconsin, Long Island
Sound in Connecticut and the bays in Massachusetts's House layer: on a 0.02° grid, every point is in one district of each level, give
or take a point on a district line. No point is in two
states where they meet. Several borders run along round degrees, such as 37°N between Utah and
Arizona, so a grid line exactly on one finds points on the line itself in neither state, or,
on 49°N, in a Washington school district and no legislative district; the tests' boxes start a
few hundredths off them.

The counties are built from the same census blocks, so they cover the state once as well: on the
grid, every point in every state is in exactly one county. No point is in two cities or two
townships, but a city or village can be in a township. Each
point's school rows are one unified district, a high school district and the elementary district
inside it, one of those two alone, or none where TIGER defines no district: 47 points of 4,310
on a 0.1° grid over California, and on a 0.05° grid 627 of 11,513 over Arizona, 207 of 11,200
over Wyoming, 2 of 11,458 over Oregon, 1 of 10,532 over Minnesota, 3 of 4,343 over
Tennessee, 2 of 4,252 over Kentucky, and over the five Great Lakes states, mostly on the lakes,
174 of 6,296 over Illinois, 25 of 3,941 over Indiana, 1,055 of 11,404 over Michigan, 402 of 4,941
over Ohio and 1,049 of 7,701 over Wisconsin; 84 of 5,029 over Pennsylvania, on Lake Erie;
and, on the water too, 49 of 611 over Connecticut, 173 of 1,187 over Massachusetts, 93 of 952
over New Jersey, 480 of 6,238 over New York and 15 of 171 over Rhode Island; none in the other
twenty-seven states (Texas on a 0.1°
grid).

Where two covered states meet, each file is thinned on its own, so both can claim a strip a few
feet wide along the border, as two neighbouring districts can. One point of the grids falls in
one: (-94.87, 33.77), on the Red River, 7 feet inside Texas in TIGER, is 2 feet inside
Oklahoma's file, and Texas's file reaches it only by taking the nearest district within 50 feet.
The lookup answers there with whichever state's file it reads first, Oklahoma. The tests allow
such a point only within thirty feet of both states' US House edges.

## Are the plans current?

TIGERweb labels its current layers the January 1, 2026 vintage, but its 120th Congress layer has
been updated since: on 2026-10-25 it held maps signed as late as May 2026. The layers' `CDSESSN`
and `LSY` fields read 120 and 2026 in every state, so only the outlines tell. Each state's
folder holds the previous layers to compare with: `cd-119.geojson` (layer 4, the 119th Congress's
districts) and `sldu-prev.geojson` and `sldl-prev.geojson` (layers 5 and 6, the 2024 plans). On a
0.05° grid, the current US House layer puts these shares of points in a different district:

- Alabama, 15%: the Legislature's 2023 map, which the Supreme Court let the state use in 2026.
  All of Mobile is in District 1, where the court's map put part of it in District 2.
- Florida, 23%: the map signed on 2026-05-04.
- Louisiana, 23%: SB 121, signed on 2026-05-29. Shreveport is in District 4, with no diagonal
  District 6.
- Missouri, 21%: the 2025 map, which the courts kept out of 2026 (see **Missouri**).
- North Carolina 15%, Ohio 21%, Texas 17% and Utah 62%: their 2025 maps.
- Tennessee, 40%: the 2026 map, which splits Memphis between Districts 5, 8 and 9.
- Every other state, 0%. Virginia's 2026 amendment was struck down, Maryland's Question 3 would
  only allow a new map from 2028, and New York's court-ordered redraw was stayed.

Of the legislative layers only Michigan's Senate (0.9%, the Detroit-area districts redrawn by
court order) and Mississippi's Senate and House (4.1% and 0.7%, the court-ordered 2025 redraw)
changed, both the plans in use. Alabama's Senate layer is its 2021 map, which the 11th Circuit
restored for 2026, and North Dakota's is the court's 2024 map, kept for 2026. Each of these was
checked against news of the plans in use for 2026 as of 2026-10-25. Suits still open in
Louisiana, Tennessee and Alabama could change a map; checking again is a download of these three
layers and the same comparison.

## Running it

From `YourElectionMap/`, once per state:

```powershell
foreach ($state in 'al', 'ar', 'az', 'ca', 'co', 'ct', 'de', 'fl', 'ga',
           'ia', 'id', 'il', 'in', 'ks', 'ky', 'la', 'ma', 'md',
           'mi', 'mn', 'mo', 'ms', 'mt', 'nc', 'nd', 'ne', 'nj',
           'nm', 'nv', 'ny', 'oh', 'ok', 'or', 'pa', 'ri', 'sc',
           'sd', 'tn', 'tx', 'ut', 'va', 'wa', 'wi', 'wv', 'wy') {
  dotnet run --project YourElectionMap.Tools -- tiger $state `
    "D:\VisualStudio\GitProjects\YourElectionMap\Docs\Tiger\$state" `
    "YourElectionMap.Server\Districts\Data\$state-districts.json"
}
```

It prints a line per district — points in, points kept, and the name of a county, city or
school district — and a line for each local layer it does not find. It refuses to write the
file if:

- the state is not one `Tiger.cs` knows, or the folder or one of its three legislative layers is
  missing;
- a layer says it was cut short (`exceededTransferLimit`);
- a feature has no `NAME` or `GEOID`, or its `GEOID` is not in the state;
- a feature's `NAME` does not end in a district number, or a number and A, B or C, unless it is
  a state's one at-large seat or its layer is one `Tiger.cs` lists as named;
- a county, city or school district's `GEOID` is in its layer twice, or its `NAME` holds a quote
  or backslash, which the file would need escaped;
- a feature is not a Polygon or MultiPolygon, or has no shape at all;
- a legislative layer's districts are not exactly 1 to N, each district `Tiger.cs` lists as
  halved being an A and a B instead, and each it lists in three an A, a B and a C, or a named
  layer's are not N, each `GEOID` once;
- a county subdivision has no `FUNCSTAT`, or one kept as a government has no `LSADC`, or one of a
  type other than a town or township;
- a coordinate is not a longitude and latitude, as a layer fetched without `outSR=4326` would be.

Two runs of the same layers produce the same bytes apart from the date in the header. With the
local layers, Texas's file is about 32 MB, California's about 19 MB and North Carolina's about
15 MB, the last two against 7 and 6 without them. The rest run from Georgia's 17 MB, Pennsylvania's 14 and
Illinois's 13 down to Wyoming's 3, Nevada's 1.5, Rhode Island's 1.0 and Delaware's 0.9.

Another state needs its FIPS code, office names, district counts, any halved districts or
districts in three, whether its districts are named, its
word for a county, where that is not "County", added to the table in `Tiger.cs`, and its three legislative layers
downloaded into a folder of its own, with its local layers alongside them for the county, city
and school rows. The local layers need nothing else in `Tiger.cs`.
